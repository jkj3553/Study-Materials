1. Factors that make a good agile team

  • Small, co-located team — typically 5–9 members for fast communication.
  • Cross-functional skills — members can design, code, test and deploy without external hand-offs.
  • Trust and self-organization — the team decides how to do the work; management sets goals, not methods.
  • Strong customer/product-owner involvement — continuous feedback shapes the backlog.
  • Shared understanding of goals — everyone knows the sprint/release objective.
  • Good communication culture — daily stand-ups, retrospectives, pair programming.
  • Stability of membership — teams that stay together sprint after sprint build tacit knowledge and velocity.
  • Discipline in engineering practice — TDD, CI/CD, refactoring, coding standards.
  • Empowerment and psychological safety — members can raise problems without fear.

2. Bad factors that lead to software project failure

  • Unrealistic schedules/budgets imposed without proper estimation.
  • Poor requirements management — incomplete, ambiguous or constantly changing requirements (scope creep).
  • Weak project planning and risk management — no contingency for risks.
  • Lack of user/stakeholder involvement throughout development.
  • Inadequate communication among team members, and between team and customer.
  • Insufficient testing and quality assurance, leading to late defect discovery.
  • Poor team skills or high staff turnover.
  • Ineffective project management — no tracking of progress, milestones or budget.
  • Technology mismatch — choosing unsuitable tools/platforms.
  • Lack of top management support and sponsorship.

3. Critical practices in software project management

  • Project planning — defining scope, schedule, resources and budget.
  • Risk management — identifying, analysing and mitigating risks continuously.
  • Requirements management — capturing, tracing and controlling change of requirements.
  • Estimation — realistic effort/cost/size estimation (FP, COCOMO, etc.).
  • Quality assurance — reviews, testing strategy, defined quality metrics.
  • Configuration management — version control of code, docs and baselines.
  • Progress monitoring and control — tracking milestones, EVM, status reporting.
  • Team management — staffing, motivation, communication and conflict resolution.
  • Stakeholder management — keeping sponsors, users and vendors aligned.
  • Change and configuration control — managing scope/requirement changes formally.

5. Role of data dictionary in a Car Parts system

In a Car Parts inventory/sales system, the data dictionary centralises definitions of every entity (Part, Supplier, Vehicle Model, Order, Customer) so that all documentation, code and database schema stay consistent.

  • Eliminates ambiguity — e.g. "PartNo" is defined once (Alphanumeric, 8 characters) and used identically everywhere.
  • Supports database design — becomes the basis for table/attribute definitions and normalisation.
  • Impact analysis — when a part attribute changes, the dictionary shows every process/report that uses it.
  • Communication tool between business analysts, developers and testers.
  • Data validation rules — e.g. Quantity ≥ 0, Price > 0, PartCategory ∈ {Engine, Brake, Electrical, Body}.
  • Supports maintenance — new developers can understand the data model quickly without reverse engineering code.

Typical entries: PartID, PartName, Category, CompatibleModel, SupplierID, UnitPrice, StockQuantity, ReorderLevel, WarrantyPeriod.

6. Factors considered when selecting project team structure

  • Difficulty of the problem to be solved — complex problems favour democratic/egoless structures.
  • Size of the resulting program (LOC) — larger systems may need hierarchical/controlled structures.
  • Time the team will stay together — short-lived teams favour simpler, well-defined roles.
  • Degree to which the problem can be modularised.
  • Required quality and reliability of the system being built.
  • Rigidity of the delivery date.
  • Degree of sociability (communication) required among team members.
  • Skill/experience level of team members and availability of specialists.

Common structures: Chief Programmer Team (centralized, hierarchical), Democratic/Egoless Team (flat, consensus-based), and Controlled Decentralized Team (hybrid — sub-teams led by senior programmers, coordinated by a project leader).

7. Product scope and project scope

Product scope

Describes the features, functions and characteristics of the product/service to be delivered — "what" the product is. It is defined by requirements and answers: what functionality must the software have?

Project scope

Describes the work that must be performed to deliver the product with the specified features — "how" the work will be done. It includes tasks, deliverables, deadlines, resources and budget, captured typically in a Work Breakdown Structure (WBS).

AspectProduct ScopeProject Scope
FocusFeatures/functions of deliverableWork needed to create the deliverable
Measured byRequirements, specificationsWBS, schedule, budget
CompletionVerified against requirementsVerified against project plan
Change controlRequirements change managementProject change control process

8. Class diagram with Aggregation — Petrol Bunk

Aggregation is a "has-a"/whole-part relationship where the part can exist independently of the whole (hollow diamond at the whole end).

A PetrolBunk aggregates FuelDispenser, StorageTank, Employee and PaymentCounter objects. Each part (e.g. an Employee) can exist even if the specific PetrolBunk object is deleted — it could be transferred to another bunk — which is the defining property of aggregation as opposed to composition.

9. Class diagram with Generalization — Petrol Bunk

Generalization is an "is-a" relationship shown with a hollow triangle arrow pointing to the parent (superclass).

PumpOperator, Cashier, Manager and Mechanic are specialisations of the generic Employee class. They inherit common attributes (EmployeeID, Name, Shift, Salary) and methods (login(), markAttendance()) from Employee, while adding their own specific behaviour (e.g. PumpOperator.dispenseFuel(), Cashier.collectPayment()).

10. Three system organizational styles — LIBSYS

LIBSYS is a library system that lets users search catalogues across several distributed sites and order documents.

a) Repository model

All sub-systems (cataloguing, search, loan management, member records) share a common central database (the repository). Changes made by one sub-system are immediately visible to others — efficient for data-intensive systems like LIBSYS where the catalogue is the shared resource.

b) Client-server model

The catalogue/search service runs on a server; user workstations (clients) at different library branches send search/loan requests over the network and receive responses — matches LIBSYS's distributed, multi-site access pattern.

c) Abstract machine (layered) model

LIBSYS can be organised in layers: a user-interface layer, a business-logic layer (search, reservation, fine calculation) and a data-management layer (catalogue database), each layer using only the services of the layer below it, which improves portability and separation of concerns.

11. VAF, Function Points, LOC, Effort and Cost calculation

Given: Count Total TC (UFP) = 300, Σ(fi) = 33, 1 FP = 50 LOC, Productivity = 14 FP/person-month, Labor cost = ₹90,000/month/developer.

I. Value Adjustment Factor (VAF)

VAF = 0.65 + 0.01 × Σ(fi) = 0.65 + 0.01 × 33 = 0.65 + 0.33 = 0.98

II. Function Point Estimate (FP)

FP = TC (UFP) × VAF = 300 × 0.98 = 294 Function Points

III. Size in LOC

Size (LOC) = FP × (LOC per FP) = 294 × 50 = 14,700 LOC

IV. Effort required (person-months)

Effort = FP / Productivity = 294 / 14 = 21 person-months

V. Project Cost — FP method and LOC method

Cost (FP method) = Effort × Labor cost per month = 21 × ₹90,000 = ₹18,90,000 For the LOC method, productivity in LOC/person-month: = 14 FP/pm × 50 LOC/FP = 700 LOC/person-month Effort (LOC method) = Size(LOC) / Productivity(LOC/pm) = 14,700 / 700 = 21 person-months Cost (LOC method) = 21 × ₹90,000 = ₹18,90,000

Result: Both methods give the same effort (21 person-months) and the same total project cost of ₹18,90,000, since LOC was derived consistently from the FP estimate.

12. State machine model — Hair Dryer Machine

States: Off → Idle (Low Heat) → Heating (High Heat) → Cooling → Off.
Events/transitions: plugIn/switchOn (Off→Idle), pressHeatButton (Idle→Heating), thermalCutoff/overheat (Heating→Idle, safety), coolButton (Heating/Idle→Cooling), switchOff (any state→Off), unplug (Off, final).
This models the dryer's reactive behaviour purely in terms of states and the events that cause transitions between them, which is the essence of a state machine (statechart) diagram.

13. Object models — explanation with diagram

An object model represents the static structure of a system in terms of objects, their classes, attributes, operations and the relationships (association, aggregation, generalization, dependency) among them. It answers "what are the things in the system and how are they related?" — as opposed to dynamic models (state/sequence) which capture behaviour over time.

Object models are usually depicted as UML class diagrams showing classes as boxes (name, attributes, operations) and lines showing multiplicities/relationships, e.g. a Customer places one or more Orders, each containing one or more Product line items. They form the foundation on which dynamic models (sequence, state) are then built.

16. Multiple inheritance with hierarchy diagram

Multiple inheritance allows a class to inherit attributes/methods from more than one parent class, combining behaviour from separate hierarchies.

Example: class FlyingCar inherits from both FlyingObject (fly(), land()) and MotorVehicle (drive(), refuel()), so a FlyingCar object has capabilities of both parents. Languages like C++ support this directly; Java avoids ambiguity (the "diamond problem") by supporting multiple inheritance of interfaces only, not implementation.

17. CASE workbenches

18. Architectural system characteristics

  • Performance — minimizing communication, localizing critical operations in few, large components.
  • Security — organizing the system into layers, protecting critical assets in innermost layers.
  • Safety — critical safety features localized in a minimal number of modules for easier verification.
  • Availability — including redundant components so the system can continue if one component fails.
  • Maintainability — using fine-grain, self-contained components that can be changed easily.

Note that some characteristics conflict (e.g. performance often favours large components while maintainability favours small ones), so architectural design involves trading these off according to system priorities.

19. Packing robot system — block diagram

20. Architectural decisions

  • Is there a generic application architecture (e.g., reference model) that can be used? — reusing existing patterns instead of designing from scratch.
  • How will the system be distributed across processors/machines (client-server, distributed, standalone)?
  • What architectural style(s) are most appropriate — e.g. layered, repository, client-server, pipe-and-filter?
  • What approach will be used to structure the system — object-oriented, function-oriented, event-based?
  • How will the system be decomposed into modules/sub-systems and how will they interact?
  • What control strategy should be used? — centralized vs event-driven control.
  • How will the architectural design be evaluated? — against quality attributes like performance, security, maintainability.
  • How should the architecture be documented? — using views (logical, process, deployment) or standard notations (UML).

21. Film and picture library — Client-Server model

Multiple specialized servers (Catalogue Server, Image/Film Server, Print Server) each provide a distinct service. Client applications (a browser for searching the catalogue, an editing tool for viewing/annotating images) send requests over the network to the appropriate server, which retrieves data from the shared Media Store and returns it. This separates the interface (client) from the compute/data-intensive services (servers), allowing independent scaling of, e.g., the Image Server which needs far more storage and bandwidth than the Catalogue Server.

22. Control styles and types

Centralized control

  • Call-return model — top-down control, one sub-system (the "controller") calls other subroutines/subsystems and waits for a result (used in sequential, procedural systems).
  • Manager model (concurrent systems) — a central process manages the starting, stopping and coordination of other concurrent processes.

Event-based control

  • Broadcast model — an event is broadcast to all sub-systems; any sub-system that can handle the event responds. Useful when subsystems must react to changes elsewhere but the source of the event may not know which subsystems need to be informed.
  • Interrupt-driven model — used in real-time systems where interrupts are detected by an interrupt handler and immediately passed to the relevant handling process; provides very fast response but is harder to prove correct.

23. OSI model with reference architectures

The OSI (Open Systems Interconnection) reference model is a classic example of a layered/abstract-machine architecture: each layer provides services to the layer above and uses services of the layer below, without knowing the internal implementation of other layers.

  • Physical — raw bit transmission over the medium.
  • Data Link — framing, error detection, MAC addressing.
  • Network — routing and logical addressing (IP).
  • Transport — end-to-end reliable delivery (TCP/UDP).
  • Session — establishing/managing sessions between applications.
  • Presentation — data translation, encryption, compression.
  • Application — end-user services (HTTP, FTP, SMTP).

This layered reference architecture allows protocols at each layer to be changed independently, as long as the interface to adjacent layers is preserved — the same principle used in software layered architectures.

24. Characteristics of Object-Oriented Design (OOD)

  • Encapsulation — objects bundle data (attributes) and behaviour (methods), hiding internal state behind a public interface.
  • Abstraction — objects model real-world or conceptual entities, exposing only relevant details.
  • Inheritance — classes can derive from other classes, reusing and extending behaviour.
  • Polymorphism — objects of different classes can be treated through a common interface, with behaviour resolved at run time.
  • Modularity — the system is decomposed into loosely-coupled, highly-cohesive objects/classes.
  • Identity — every object has a unique identity, distinct from its state.
  • Message passing — objects interact only by sending messages (calling methods) to each other.
  • Reusability — classes and components can be reused across projects, reducing development effort.

25. Transponder object — Java code

A transponder (e.g. in an air-traffic system) receives an interrogation signal and automatically transmits identification/altitude data in response.

public class Transponder { private String aircraftID; private double altitude; private double speed; private boolean isActive; public Transponder(String aircraftID) { this.aircraftID = aircraftID; this.isActive = true; } // Called when an interrogation signal is received public String respondToInterrogation() { if (!isActive) { return "NO_RESPONSE"; } return "ID:" + aircraftID + " ALT:" + altitude + " SPD:" + speed; } public void updateAltitude(double newAltitude) { this.altitude = newAltitude; } public void updateSpeed(double newSpeed) { this.speed = newSpeed; } public void squawkEmergency() { this.aircraftID = "7700-" + aircraftID; // emergency code } public void switchOff() { this.isActive = false; } }

The class encapsulates the transponder's state (ID, altitude, speed, active flag) and exposes methods that model its real behaviour — responding automatically to interrogation and allowing updates from onboard sensors, which illustrates encapsulation and message-based interaction typical of OOD.

28. Approaches to Object Identification

  • Grammatical (noun) analysis — nouns in the requirements/problem description are candidate objects/classes, verbs suggest operations (based on Abbott's technique).
  • Use of tangible entities / behaviour analysis — identify real-world things the system must track or control (people, devices, records) and model them directly as objects.
  • Domain analysis — build on existing domain-specific object models/frameworks from similar, previously-built systems.
  • Scenario-based analysis (use-case driven) — identify objects that participate in each use-case scenario by examining the interactions needed to fulfil it.
  • CRC (Class-Responsibility-Collaboration) cards — an informal, collaborative technique where each candidate class's responsibilities and collaborators are listed on a card, aiding identification and refinement.

In practice, several approaches are combined: grammatical analysis provides an initial candidate list, which is then refined via scenario walkthroughs and CRC-card sessions.

29. Design evolution — adding a pollution monitoring feature

Design evolution is the process of extending/adapting an existing architecture to add new capability without a complete redesign — illustrated using the weather-station example extended with pollution monitoring.

  1. Identify the change — a new requirement: monitor air-pollution levels (e.g. CO₂, SO₂, particulate matter) alongside existing weather data.
  2. Assess impact on existing architecture — because the weather station is built as extensible objects/subsystems (Data Collection, Processing, Archiving), a new PollutionSensor object/subsystem can be added rather than modifying existing classes — an example of the Open/Closed principle.
  3. Extend the object model — add classes such as PollutionMonitor, GasSensor, ParticulateSensor that inherit from an existing generic Instrument/Sensor superclass, reusing common behaviour (calibrate(), readValue(), reportFault()).
  4. Extend the interfaces — the Data Collection subsystem's interface is extended to accept pollution readings alongside temperature/humidity, without changing its interface to other subsystems.
  5. Update data formats/reports — the transmitted data-report format is extended (not replaced) with new pollution fields, preserving backward compatibility with the Mapping System.
  6. Re-test and re-validate — regression-test unaffected subsystems, and specifically test the new pollution feature end to end.

This shows how good initial object-oriented design (loosely coupled subsystems, inheritance hierarchies for instruments) makes later evolution cheaper and less risky than in monolithic, tightly-coupled designs.

30. Organisational paradigms suggested by Constantine

Larry Constantine identified four organisational paradigms for software teams:

ParadigmCharacteristics
ClosedStructures the team along a traditional hierarchy of authority; efficient for well-understood, routine problems but stifles creativity.
RandomDepends on individual initiative of team members with little structure; good for highly innovative problems but can be inefficient when member skills are more or less equal.
OpenAttempts to organise teams in a manner that combines the strengths of closed and random paradigms; uses lots of communication and consensus decision-making — effective for solving complex problems.
SynchronousRelies on the natural division of labour so that team members have well-defined tasks with minimal need for communication; works well when the problem can be partitioned into independent, non-communicating tasks.

31. Different types of Estimation techniques

  • Expert judgement — one or more experts estimate effort/cost based on experience with similar past projects.
  • Estimation by analogy (analogous estimation) — compare the new project with completed similar projects and scale the known effort/cost.
  • Algorithmic/parametric models — mathematical models relating effort to project attributes: e.g. COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model), Function Point Analysis, Putnam's SLIM model.
  • Bottom-up estimation — decompose the project into small tasks (WBS), estimate each, then sum them up.
  • Top-down estimation — estimate the overall project effort first, then allocate proportions to sub-tasks/phases.
  • Delphi technique / Wideband Delphi — a structured, anonymous group estimation technique that iterates until estimates converge.
  • Planning poker (agile) — team members independently estimate relative size (story points) and discuss to reach consensus.
  • Price-to-win estimation — the estimate is set to match the customer's budget/expectation rather than a true technical estimate (a risky but sometimes used commercial practice).

Best practice is to use two or more of these techniques together and compare results, since no single technique is reliable in isolation.

32. COCOMO model

COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model), developed by Barry Boehm, estimates effort, schedule and cost based on estimated project size (KLOC — thousand lines of code).

Levels of COCOMO

  • Basic COCOMO — a simple, single-value estimate of effort as a function of program size alone.
  • Intermediate COCOMO — computes effort as a function of program size and a set of "cost drivers" (attributes like reliability, complexity, personnel experience, tool use).
  • Detailed (Advanced) COCOMO — incorporates the effect of the cost drivers on each phase (analysis, design, coding, testing) of the software life cycle.

Project modes (Basic COCOMO)

ModeDescriptionEffort Formula (E, in person-months)
OrganicSmall, familiar team, well-understood problemE = 2.4 × (KLOC)1.05
Semi-detachedMedium size/complexity, mixed experienceE = 3.0 × (KLOC)1.12
EmbeddedComplex, tight constraints (hardware/regulatory)E = 3.6 × (KLOC)1.20

Development time is then estimated as D = a × E^b (constants depending on the mode), and average staffing as Effort / Development time. COCOMO is widely used because it gives quick, size-based estimates early in the project, though its accuracy depends heavily on getting a good KLOC estimate.
