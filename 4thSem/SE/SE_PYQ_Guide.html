Question 1. a)

Discuss the responsibilities that the software engineers should have towards professional and society. State ACM/IEEE principles that software engineers should adhere to the code of Ethics and professional practice. [6 Marks]

1. General Professional & Social Responsibilities

Software engineering is carried out within a legal and social framework. Software engineers must display ethical behavior, honesty, and integrity rather than simply focusing on technical competence. They have professional responsibilities that go beyond the technical code, including:

  • Confidentiality: Engineers must respect the confidentiality of employers and clients, regardless of whether a formal confidentiality agreement has been executed.
  • Competence: Engineers must not misrepresent their level of competence or knowingly accept work that is outside their current expertise.
  • Intellectual Property Rights: Engineers must be aware of and abide by local and international laws governing patents, copyrights, and intellectual property.
  • Computer Misuse: Software engineers must not deploy their technical skills to intentionally misuse or compromise other computer systems.

2. ACM/IEEE Code of Ethics Principles

The joint ACM/IEEE-CS task force formulated a standard Code of Ethics composed of 8 core principles:

  1. PUBLIC: Software engineers shall act consistently with the public interest.
  2. CLIENT AND EMPLOYER: Software engineers shall act in a manner that is in the best interests of their client and employer, consistent with the public interest.
  3. PRODUCT: Software engineers shall ensure that their products and related modifications meet the highest professional standards possible.
  4. JUDGMENT: Software engineers shall maintain integrity and independence in their professional judgment.
  5. MANAGEMENT: Software engineering managers and leaders shall subscribe to and promote an ethical approach to the management of software development and maintenance.
  6. PROFESSION: Software engineers shall advance the integrity and reputation of the profession consistent with the public interest.
  7. COLLEAGUES: Software engineers shall be fair to and supportive of their colleagues.
  8. SELF: Software engineers shall participate in lifelong learning regarding the practice of their profession and shall promote an ethical approach to the practice of the profession.

Question 2. c)

Illustrate the problems of using natural language for defining user and system requirements, using the example for Insulin Pump Control Software. Explain how structuring natural language into forms can help avoid some of these difficulties. [8 Marks]

1. Core Problems of Using Unstructured Natural Language

Using unstructured natural language for software specifications introduces several challenges:

  • Lack of Clarity: Natural language statements can be imprecise and ambiguous, making it difficult to write definitions with technical accuracy.
  • Requirements Confusion: Functional requirements (system capabilities), non-functional requirements (operating bounds), and system design information can become intermingled, confusing implementations.
  • Requirements Amalgamation: Multiple distinct requirements may be combined into a single paragraph, making tracking, verification, and verification difficult.

2. Insulin Pump Example Scenario (Unstructured Text Fallacy)

An unstructured text entry might state:

"The software must calculate blood sugar variations continuously and distribute insulin safely via the pump infrastructure while raising an alert if levels spike dangerously or hardware malfunctions occur."

  • Critique: This statement mixes computation functions (calculate variations), physical actuation functions (distribute insulin), safety limits (safely), and distinct error handling rules (sugar spike vs. hardware fault) into a single block. It does not define what a "dangerous spike" means quantitatively, leaving room for interpretation.

3. How Structured Forms Resolve These Difficulties

Structuring requirements into standardized templates restricts unconstrained text to a predefined input template. This ensures consistency, simplifies cross-referencing, and forces the author to think about the inputs, operational bounds, and side effects of each requirement.

Structured Template Definition for the Insulin Pump Software:

  • Function Identifier: SYS-RE-IP-04
  • Description: Calculate and deliver the required insulin dose based on measured blood sugar levels.
  • Inputs: Measured Blood Sugar Level ($Sugar_{current}$), Prior Blood Sugar Level ($Sugar_{previous}$), Active Insulin Level ($Insulin_{active}$).
  • Source: Subcutaneous sensor hardware array.
  • Outputs: Actuation signal to stepper motor ($Pulse_{count}$).
  • Destination: Insulin pump micro-injector hardware mechanism.
  • Action Context: The software calculates the change in glucose concentration over time. If levels rise, it determines the basal dose required to stabilize blood sugar. The total calculated output step must never exceed the absolute safe limit defined in the safety specification.
  • Pre-condition: The insulin reservoir must contain a minimum volume threshold of fluid, and the sensor must clear its diagnostic self-test.
  • Post-condition: The internal historical ledger logs the precise milligram dosage delivered, and the target delivery counter updates.
  • Side-Effects/Exceptions: If the calculation yields a value outside the safe operational limits, or if a hardware sensor fault is detected, the delivery mechanism locks, the software switches to fail-safe mode, and a physical audio alarm is activated.

Question 3. a) ii)

Illustrate the four important attributes which all software products should have. Identify and Suggest four other attributes that may sometimes be significant for a Fire Alarm system. [4 Marks]

1. Four Essential Attributes of All Software Products

  1. Maintainability: Software should be written so that it can evolve to meet the changing needs of customers without requiring a complete rewrite.
  2. Dependability & Security: Software must be reliable, secure, and safe, ensuring it does not cause physical or economic damage in the event of system failure or malicious attacks.
  3. Efficiency: Software should optimize the utilization of system resources, such as CPU cycles, memory allocation, and storage footprints.
  4. Acceptability: The software must be understandable, usable, and compatible with other operational systems used by its target audience.

2. Four Critical Attributes Specific to a Fire Alarm System

  1. Real-Time Responsiveness (Latency Bounds): The system must guarantee that sensor polling inputs showing thermal spikes trigger alarm notifications within a strict maximum latency window (e.g., $<200$ milliseconds).
  2. Fail-Safe Fault Tolerance: If a sensor component breaks or loses power, the software must drop into a fallback mode that flags the issue via a local siren rather than failing silently.
  3. Robustness Against Environmental Interference: The processing algorithms must filter out environmental noise (such as dust buildup or localized cooking steam) to avoid triggering false positive alarms.
  4. Resilience to Security Attacks (Tamper Proofing): The embedded firmware must resist external tampering, unauthorized network connections, and denial-of-service (DoS) attempts to prevent malicious actors from disabling the alarm infrastructure.

Question 5. b)

Illustrate the two fundamental types of evolutionary development. Identify the problems existing in an evolutionary approach. [6 Marks]

Evolutionary development is based on the idea of developing an initial implementation, exposing it to user comment, and refining it through many versions until an acceptable system is produced.

1. Two Fundamental Types of Evolutionary Development

  1. Exploratory Development: The objective of this process is to work directly with the customer to explore their requirements and deliver a final, operational system. Development starts with the system parts that are well understood. The system evolves by adding new features as proposed by the customer.
  2. Throw-away Prototyping: The objective of this process is to illustrate and clarify poorly understood requirements to gain a better understanding of what the system needs. The prototype focuses on experimenting with requirements that are unclear or risky. Once requirements are clarified, this experimental software build is discarded, and the actual system is rewritten from scratch using a more structured process model.

2. Critical Problems Existing in the Evolutionary Approach

From a managerial and architectural perspective, evolutionary development introduces several notable drawbacks:

  • Lack of Process Visibility: Software systems evolve rapidly through multiple design iterations. It is not cost-effective for managers to produce comprehensive documentation, design models, or specification revisions for every single intermediate release.
  • Poor System Structure: Continuous changes to code modules introduce architectural erosion. Because new features are integrated ad-hoc into existing software builds, modules become tightly coupled, making long-term maintenance difficult and expensive.
  • Special Skills Requirement: Effective evolutionary development requires experienced engineers who can navigate fluid requirements and use rapid prototyping tools, a skillset that can be harder to find than standard development competencies.

Question 1.1

Analyze why design conflicts might arise when designing an architecture where availability and security requirements are the most important non-functional/functional requirements. Justify your answer. [4 Marks]

Exam-Perfect Answer:

Architectural design is a balancing act where optimization for one non-functional quality attribute often directly degrades another. When availability and security are simultaneously established as the highest priorities, structural engineering conflicts arise for the following reasons:

  1. Redundancy vs. Attack Surface Expansion (Availability vs. Security):
  2. Availability Strategy: To guarantee high availability, architectural patterns dictate the inclusion of redundant components (e.g., multi-node server clusters, replicated databases, and redundant communication paths). If one node fails, failover subsystems keep operations running.

  3. Security Conflict: Every single redundant component, mirrored database link, and backup data path added to the topology introduces a new potential vulnerability and entry point for an adversary to target. Managing authorization matrices and patch levels across multiple data stores increases the risk of configuration errors and security leaks.

  4. Performance Latency Bounds vs. Cryptographic Verification (Availability vs. Security):

  5. Availability Strategy: Availability relies on rapid subsystem responses, load-balancing checks, and minimal latency bounds to maintain data flow and prevent service timeouts.

  6. Security Conflict: High-level security requires multiple layers of authentication, token checks, parameter sanitization, and complex data encryption/decryption sequences (e.g., TLS handshakes, AES-256 block ciphers) at every layer. These necessary security controls consume CPU cycles and expand data transmission times, increasing transaction latencies. This overhead can make the system more vulnerable to Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks that exhaust server resources.

  7. Loose Access Authorization vs. Least Privilege Monitored Latching:

  8. High availability requires clear fallback paths, meaning that if a central user-directory server goes down, local modules should use cached credentials to avoid system downtime. Conversely, strict security practices require continuous validation against a central server to ensure privileges haven't been revoked, making fallback caching an intentional risk.

Question 6.1

What types of risks are likely to be encountered as the software is being built, and as the technical leader, how do you assess the overall risk associated with the software? [8 Marks]

Exam-Perfect Answer:
1. Core Classifications of Software Risks

Software engineering projects face three main categories of risks:

  • Project Risks: Threats that directly jeopardize the project schedule, resource allocation, or cost models. Examples include high staff turnover, budget cuts, or losing key development resources.
  • Technical Risks: Threats that compromise the quality, performance, or long-term maintainability of the software product. These typically stem from using unproven technologies, facing complex design bottlenecks, or dealing with poorly understood requirements patterns.
  • Business Risks: Threats that undermine the commercial viability or market adoption of the product. These include building a product that no longer fits the company's core sales strategy, losing executive management support, or encountering new market competitors.
2. Methodologies for Assessing Overall Project Risk

To evaluate risk levels across the lifecycle, a technical leader follows a structured two-step process:

  • Step A: Risk Identification using a Risk Item Checklist:
    The leader screens the project environment against established risk factors by asking targeted diagnostic questions:
  • Management Commitment: Have senior management and customer leadership formally committed to supporting the project?
  • Requirements Stability: Are user requirements fully understood by the engineering team, and is the project scope stable?
  • Skills Integration: Does the development team possess the right mix of technical skills and experience required for the chosen technology stack?

  • Staffing Adequacy: Is the number of people assigned to the project sufficient to complete the work within the scheduled timeline?

  • Step B: Quantifying Risk Exposure ($RE$):
    For each identified risk, the leader estimates its probability of occurrence ($P$) and its total impact or cost ($C$) if it manifests. Risk Exposure is calculated using the following equation:

$$RE = P \times C$$

  • Example Analysis: Suppose there is an $80\%$ probability ($P = 0.80$) that a third-party software library component will fail integration, requiring an estimated $18$ person-days of rework across $100$ components at an engineering cost rate of $\$14.00/\text{LOC}$.
  • Impact Cost ($C$): $18 \times 100 \times \$14.00 = \$25,200$.
  • Risk Exposure ($RE$): $0.80 \times \$25,200 \approx \mathbf{\$20,160}$.

By summing and ranking the risk exposure values across all items, the manager builds a prioritized checklist to guide risk mitigation efforts.

They design specific test inputs to guarantee that every branch of the internal logical conditions (if salary > 45000, else if salary < 15000) executes at least once, verifying that variables are initialized correctly inside the execution blocks.

Exam-Perfect Answer:
1. Theoretical Principles of Equivalence Partitioning

Equivalence partitioning divides a program's input data domains into distinct classes of data from which test cases can be derived. It assumes that all values within a given partition will cause the software to behave in the same way. Therefore, testing a single representative value from each partition is sufficient to validate that data group. Partitions are split into Valid Classes (inputs the system should process successfully) and Invalid Classes (inputs the system should reject with an error message).
